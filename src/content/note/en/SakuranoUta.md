> At the end of the poem left by the artist of the cherry blossoms alongside his paintings, it was inscribed: The artist dies, and in his wake, the landscape of ordinary people's ordinary happiness unfolds.
>
> The sound of the world echoes through the gently swaying forest of cherry blossoms, as graceful melodies play the voice of the world.

In the circle of GalGame enthusiasts, few have likely never heard of this masterpiece: *Sakura no Uta -The tear flows because of tenderness.-* (サクラノ詩). It is an aesthetic monument hailed by countless players as a "God-tier" work.

Having finally found some leisure time recently, I spent a month immersing myself in and completing the entire story. I record my reflections and resonances here, serving as a footnote to this long journey.

## Frühlingsbeginn

![](https://static.maredevi.fun/2026-04-22-104920_hyprshot.png)  

"Frühlingsbeginn" is German for "Beginning of Spring." Using it as the title of the first chapter is perfectly apt. But what left a deeper impression on me was the quote that leaped onto the screen at the start of Chapter I:

> "When the one you love dies, you must commit suicide."
> "When the one you love dies, there is no other way but that."

These are lines from the Japanese poet Chuya Nakahara’s *Spring Rhapsody* (Naoya’s artwork hung in the city hall is also titled *Spring Rhapsody*). Having walked through the entire plot, I think I understand why SCA-DI placed this poem at the very beginning of the story: **"The self of the past must die to reconstruct the soul as a newborn who carries the will of the deceased."**

The plot of Chapter I is relatively gentle, leaning towards campus daily life (its overall atmosphere does bear some resemblance to *I Want to Convey My Love to You*). Its main role is to introduce the core characters while burying foreshadowing for the vast, intersecting network of fates that follows.

## Abend

"Abend" is German for "Evening." As the routes diverge, the plot varies slightly, but the pointers eventually converge at one point: Naoya and the members of the Art Club jointly completing Kusanagi Kenichiro’s posthumous work, *Footsteps of the Cherry Blossoms*. As its chapter name metaphors, the story of Chapter II is a sunset-like, beautiful yet melancholic daily life at Yumihari Academy that is destined to end.

## III

### Olympia

![](https://static.maredevi.fun/2026-04-22-112639_hyprshot.png)

"Olympia" is the name of an automaton in the opera *The Tales of Hoffmann*, and it is also the name of the dollhouse in the game.

When young Rin was on the brink of a mental breakdown after her mother passed away, she regarded the doll named "Sui" as her mother. In *The Tales of Hoffmann*, the protagonist falls in love with the automaton Olympia and obsessively insists she is a living person. Rin is the same; she pushes Olympia in a wheelchair for walks and buys clothes for it—this is essentially a long nightmare that refuses to wake up. For Rin, as long as Olympia is still "Mother," the reality that "Mother died because of me"—a reality sufficient to destroy her mind—would not completely crush her.

And for Naoya, Olympia is also the coordinate of his self-sacrifice. This also uncovers the biggest suspense buried in the first two chapters: Why did Naoya, hailed as a genius, suddenly stop painting? It was because in that fire, in order to catch Rin who was falling from a high building, his right hand was completely destroyed, and he fell from the altar of genius. After the fire, Naoya cooperated with Rin's father to bury the truth of the fire in order to protect Rin's then-fragile spirit. Guarding this phantom named "Olympia" was guarding Rin's soul from being devoured by the karmic fire of guilt.

This actually constructs a cross-time intertextuality with the story of Chapter IV: The father, Kenichiro, uses the painting *Olympia* to save a real life; while Naoya, by discarding art (stopping painting), desperately guards a false life about "Olympia." This is the father and son reaching the same destination through different paths on the road of artistic pursuit—**Life is always more important than art.**

Regarding Sui, she is the spiritualization under the miracle of the Millennium Cherry Blossom. Kenichiro once evaluated Rin as a "genius favored by God," possessing the ability to create something out of nothing. After the fire, Rin lost this talent, and this part of her talent (as well as her deep yearning for her mother) was lodged in the doll Olympia, turning into the girl "Sui." This explains why she showed artistic power beyond common sense when assisting Naoya in completing *Footsteps of the Cherry Blossoms*. She is the projection of **"Misaki Rin as she should have been as an artist."**

When Rin finally retrieves her memories, faces the truth of the fire, and reaches a reconciliation with her father, Olympia's mission as a "substitute" comes to an end. The dissipation of the girl "Sui" marks Rin truly awakening from the "phantom dream of the doll" and beginning to breathe again as a "human." And what Sui had been painstakingly searching for was precisely the shards of Rin's shattered soul from the fire years ago. When Sui finally merged into Rin's body, Rin not only regained her memories but also rediscovered that "Platinum Award" level of peerless talent. The thing Sui spent a whole chapter looking for, once found, meant her extinction as an independent individual.

> "What she was looking for, in fact, was the reason for her own disappearance."

### PicaPica

Of all the routes, Makoto's route should be the one closest to reality and the most simple. If Misaki Rin's route is exploring "miracles and salvation," then Makoto's route is depicting **"how an ordinary person should face the shadow of a genius and achieve harmony with it."**

"PicaPica" is the scientific name for the magpie. In Western contexts, magpies are believed to have the habit of "collecting shiny things." The core imagery of this chapter revolves around Monet's *The Magpie* and the derived "Magpie Vase," which exquisitely metaphors the character of Makoto: although she considers herself an "ordinary person," she has spent half her life chasing, collecting, and guarding those who shine with the light of genius (Naoya and Kei). The magpie is also a "mimic." Makoto making pottery, collecting paintings of geniuses, and even Toriya Saki fabricating the grand lie about Monet, are all like the magpie, trying to construct a nest to protect geniuses with "forged brilliance."

As the daughter of Principal Toriya and the half-sister of Kei, Makoto is surrounded by geniuses at the pinnacle. Although Makoto and Kei carry the blood of the prestigious Nakamura family, due to the complex entanglement between their mother Toriya Saki and the family, the two siblings' childhood was actually spent in "house arrest" and isolation.

In that cold mansion full of tedious etiquette, painting became the only common language between the two siblings. In that room called the "secret base," facing the walls and their wild imagination, they drew the ladder leading to the moon. The ladder symbolizes escape, and the moon in their eyes is the "outside world," the utopia for breaking free from the heavy bloodline shackles of the Nakamura family.

At that time, Makoto recognized her own position: Kei is the one with amazing talent who can keep climbing up. In Makoto's eyes, Kei's back is always moving towards that unattainable moon of art. Makoto knew she didn't have Kei's talent, so she chose to stand at the bottom of the ladder to steady it for Kei.
This bond continued after they grew up. The reason why Makoto desperately guarded the Art Club and even wanted to bring Naoya back at all costs was essentially that she was still **"holding the ladder"** in her adulthood—she vainly tried to build a bridge to the divine realm for these two geniuses (Naoya and Kei).

When Toriya Saki broke with Nakamura Shoichi, she took Makoto but left Kei alone. For Makoto, this decision was equivalent to her withdrawing the ladder alone, abandoning Kei in the dark abyss of the Nakamura family. Much of Makoto's later almost obsessive "spirit of self-sacrifice" was born from this deep-seated guilt. She obsessively believed that the reason Kei couldn't paint the "soul" later was precisely because she abandoned him that night. Therefore, the pottery she fires day after day is actually trying to reassemble that shattered "moon."

At the end of this chapter, Makoto fires the work she is most satisfied with in her life—a pottery jar named "Moon." The moon does not shine on its own; Makoto calmly accepts that her art, and even her life, are reflecting the brilliance of Naoya and Kei. The metaphor of "the moon and the ladder" reaches a sublimation at this moment: Makoto no longer hopes to climb that moon belonging exclusively to geniuses, but chooses to be down-to-earth and reflect their light in her own way (pottery, life, love).
At the end of Makoto's route, although Naoya refuses to return to the throne of "genius," he chooses to spend the rest of his life with Makoto, which actually gives that childhood metaphor the most warmth-filled realistic answer:  
They no longer need that illusory moon hanging high in the sky. **If the ladder can't reach the moon, then embracing under the ladder is also a form of happiness.**

![](https://static.maredevi.fun/2026-04-22-114632_hyprshot.png)

Regarding Toriya Saki, the depiction of her in this chapter is penetrating. She is a resilient woman who armed herself with a heart of stone to fight against the decayed Nakamura family and protect her children.
Saki forged the newspaper clipping about Monet, a grand lie that deceived experts worldwide but only failed to deceive Kusanagi Kenichiro. This "aesthetic gamble" reveals a very profound truth: **The value of art sometimes lies not in the verification of authenticity, but in the "will" it carries.**
She sent Kei away to protect him from the mental pollution of the Nakamura family, but this extreme protective umbrella resulted in Makoto's ten years of hatred. One of the most shocking climaxes of Makoto's route is precisely these two generations of women finally reaching a cross-century mother-daughter reconciliation through the "gamble of art."

![](https://static.maredevi.fun/2026-04-22-114723_hyprshot.png)

### ZYPRESSEN

"ZYPRESSEN" is taken from the German for "Cypresses." In art history (especially in Van Gogh's work) and Western cultural contexts, cypresses are often totems of death, mourning, and eternity. This forms a sharp and tragic binary opposition with the "cherry blossoms" that represent ephemeral brilliance and the cycle of life.

The conflict in this chapter is, at its root, a life-and-death gamble between two aesthetics and views of life:

- **Rina's "Breath of Death":** Suffering from severe ultraviolet allergy, Rina was heartlessly deprived of the right to breathe in the sunlight. She could only wander in the dark night, which made her inner self resonate with the "cypresses" symbolizing death. In the graffiti of Cypress Park, Rina's paintings are hollow and permeated with a desire for destruction.
- **Naoya's "Giving of Life":** Although Naoya at that time had almost no possibility of painting due to his disability, his deep affection for Rina (and his remaining artistic intuition) drove him to choose to redraw "life" upon Rina's "death." Upon that cypress tree symbolizing death, he splashed wildly blooming cherry blossoms.

Such an achievement has already transcended simple artistic creation; it is more like an empowerment and pouring of life force. Naoya "sacrificed" all of his last remaining artistic talent capable of touching the divine realm to Rina, thereby dispelling the deathly air surrounding her. This also explains why Naoya is completely unable to paint in other routes, but in Rina's route, this "sacrifice" is endowed with a more tragic sense of contract.

The most amazing part of this chapter is that it is by no means just a song for the pure love between the male and female protagonists, but a tug-of-war of the complex **"salvation and bondage"** among Naoya, Rina, and Kawauchiya Yumi.
Rina is not a purely "weak girl." She keenly smelled Yumi's obsession with her and used this tenderness to "bind" Yumi to her side. This is a kind of love born of extreme loneliness with a selfish undertone.
Yumi is one of the most lamentable characters in this chapter. She loves Rina deeply, but her love is deformed and twisted—she both longs to see Rina reborn and, in the depths of her subconscious, prays that Rina will always be trapped in that cold corner where only the two of them exist. But when Yumi witnesses the "Cherry Cypress" Naoya painted for Rina, she realizes: the thing Naoya can give Rina (embracing a bright future) is something she could never give even if she spent her whole life.
Naoya showed a very strong "Motherly nature"; by sacrificing the future of his art, he exchanged a redemption ticket back to normal life for a girl deeply trapped in a quagmire.

The ancient legend of the Baku and Yoshisada introduced in this chapter is actually a fateful reflection of the past and present lives of Rina and Yumi.
The Baku developed human emotions for Yoshisada and finally moved towards extinction because it could not devour the nightmare; this corresponds to Rina's struggle to walk out of the haze for Naoya, and Yumi's sacrifice of finally cutting off her possessiveness for Rina. When the Millennium Cherry Blossom blooms poignantly at the shrine, it senses the sharp pain in the depths of Yumi's soul. If Yumi made a wish, she would be fully capable of making Naoya disappear into thin air or keeping Rina by her side forever. But Yumi finally chose to let go. She finally understood that true love is not about imprisoning her in the cypress forest, but watching her breathe freely under the cherry blossom tree. **The Millennium Cherry Blossom here is not only the embodiment of a miracle but also the most cruel touchstone of the brilliance of human nature.**

The aftertaste of Rina's route is extremely long: Naoya and Rina forged a contract based on "absolute devotion." Naoya no longer covets the painting skills of the divine realm, but chooses to shed his halo and accompany her as an ordinary guardian.
The final frame of the ending is extremely jarring: Rina steps into Naoya's flourishing world of cherry blossoms, while Yumi is left alone in that black cypress forest that nurtured their bond but will forever emit the breath of death and loneliness.

### A Nice Derangement of Epitaphs

"A Nice Derangement of Epitaphs." This is my most preferred chapter in the entire plot. If the previous routes were delicately laying out the personal struggles and emotional salvation of each character, then this chapter is a heavy hammer, completely smashing the shackles of fate that have entangled the Kusanagi, Natsume, and Nakamura families for decades, while also pushing the grand themes of "art, miracles, and sacrifice" to their final song.

The core conflict of Shizuku's route is, on the surface, Naoya's life-and-death gamble with the head of the Nakamura family (Nakamura Shoichi) to protect Shizuku, but its undertone is a tragic microcosm of three generations of the Kusanagi and Natsume families fighting against the Nakamura family's distorted system (Umoregi-sha).
In order to continue the pure blood of the "Hoso-shi clan," the Nakamura family used extremely anti-human inbreeding to produce the "Baku" with the ability to eat dreams. This is not just feudal poison, but the most outrageous objectification and deprivation of female individuals (such as Mizuna, Ai, and Shizuku). Kotoko spent her family wealth to buy Umoregi-sha and convert it into the Natsume residence, and Kenichiro even used the price of forging Manet's famous paintings as a bargaining chip. Although they saved some victims, they ultimately failed to redeem Mizuna, whom Kenichiro loved most, and could not take away Shizuku, the true "Baku." For this reason, Kenichiro went far away and spent his whole life turning his paintbrush into a blade (accumulating huge wealth) to fight against this huge, cold-blooded machine.

Naoya inherited his father's unfinished madness. He gave up the huge inheritance of 1.5 billion, not simply because of the rift between father and son, but to protect Shizuku in the undercurrent; he even did not hesitate to dirty his hands that once held the paintbrush, forging fakes of **"Sakura Shichisou-zu"** to fill Shizuku's bottomless debt.
What is shown here is both Kenichiro's supreme aesthetics pursuing "emptiness and simplicity," and the "bargaining chip" used to buy freedom and fight against power in the dirty quagmire of reality. Although Naoya's forgery violated the absolute taboos of the art world, at this very moment, it transformed into the most noble and tragic salvation.

This chapter extremely exquisitely closes the existence loop where Natsume Shizuku, Misaki Rin, and Sui are causes and effects for each other.
As a pure Baku, Shizuku should have been deprived of all emotions (mindless). But Kenichiro, by bringing the genius girl Rin to her side, forcibly "painted" a beating human heart for Shizuku with Rin's terrifying infectious power of "materializing imagination."
In order to bring her mother back to life, Rin tried to forcibly twist the miracle of the "Millennium Cherry Blossom." But the abuse of miracles is often accompanied by destructive backlash. To stop Rin's rampage, Shizuku used her power as a Baku and swallowed Rin's dream in one gulp. The price was that Rin lost her shocking artistic talent and her memory of her mother, becoming an "ordinary girl." And Rin's overflowing talent and intense yearning that had nowhere to go eventually materialized into "Sui." The birth of Sui was both Rin's forcibly sealed past and a dangerous balance that maintained the current state of this broken world.
Miracles are never given a warm filter in *Sakura no Uta*; their arrival is often accompanied by equivalent or even more tragic deprivation. Naoya and Kenichiro's silence about all this, and even Naoya's desperate cessation of painting, were all to support this crumbling reality of "using one miracle to cover another miracle," just to protect these girls who were already scarred.

![](https://static.maredevi.fun/2026-04-22-121144_hyprshot.png)

Looking at the title again, "A Nice Derangement of Epitaphs." In the dust-covered old studio of the Natsume family, Naoya used mechanisms to copy his father's brushwork and burned his heart's blood to paint six fakes within three weeks. When he completed the last scroll, Kenichiro's phantom appeared through life and death and personally inscribed the six paintings.
These six paintings were never despicable fakes made to deceive the world from the beginning, but the son Naoya's deepest mourning and epitaph for his deceased father, for which he gave everything. Kenichiro failed to save enough money to redeem Shizuku during his lifetime, and Naoya used these paintings to complete the last leg of the journey for his father. At this moment, father and son achieved complete reconciliation in the depths of their souls through cooperation across the boundaries of life and death.

In this route, the bottom card of the Millennium Cherry Blossom was also completely revealed. It is by no means an Aladdin's lamp that can unconditionally grant wishes, but a huge container that absorbs human fanatical yearning. The Millennium Cherry Blossom in the past only brought distortion (sealing Rin's memory, tearing families apart). But at the end, in order not to let Sui vanish into nothingness, Naoya resolutely took up the paintbrush again. Naoya at this time had transformed into the "absolute core attracting the light of everyone's karma." He was no longer that cowardly boy escaping in the shadows; he was going to use his own paintbrush to harness and guide the huge miracle of the Millennium Cherry Blossom into the right track.

**True exquisite beauty and miracles are often built on the ruins of huge sacrifice, deception, and blood.** This father and son, Kenichiro and Naoya, with their burned-out lives and art, forcibly tore open a path to light for their beloved women from this cruel and ruthless net of fate. Naoya finally chose to paint again, marking his complete transcendence over the nightmares of the past and his growth into a man truly capable of carrying the fate of others.

## What is mind? No matter. What is matter? Never mind

This chapter is not only a look back at Kusanagi Kenichiro's past but also the spiritual high ground where the core contradiction of **"art and reality, matter and spirit"** is discussed in the whole work.

> "What is mind? No matter." (What is mind? Matter doesn't exist / It's irrelevant.)  
> "What is matter? Never mind." (What is matter? Spirit is absent / Don't pay it any mind.)

This chapter turns back the clock several decades. At that time, Yumihari Academy was tightly enveloped by the decayed Nakamura family and its clannish power.
Kenichiro was a "game-breaker" who broke conventions and was completely unfettered by mundane rules. His arrival was like a very intense and abrupt color, splashed into the grayish Yumihari City.
Mizuna and Ai were not well-bred young ladies living in luxury, but "descendants of concubines" imprisoned in "Umoregi-sha" and regarded as breeding machines and playthings. This sickening family tradition was precisely the most extreme strangulation of the human **Mind** by the **Matter** world.

The process of Kenichiro pulling Mizuna out of hell was essentially a "fraudulent rebellion" of art against the cold reality:
Manet's famous painting *Olympia* has revolutionary significance in art history for breaking traditional shackles. By copying the Mizuna version of *Olympia*, Kenichiro used the premium and fame hyped from the artwork as a bargaining chip to conduct an absurd "redemption" deal with the Nakamura family. This perfectly fits the title of this chapter: he used the **Mind** that transcends the mundane world to forcibly settle the dirtiest **Matter** debt.

This chapter also completes the puzzle of Saki. She loved Kenichiro deeply but was forced by family pressure to submit to Nakamura Shoichi. She spent her whole life wandering on the edge of the "rules," trying to protect Kenichiro with material means, but Kenichiro chose the most dangerous and romantic elopement. This also explains why, many years later, she would show such a contradictory emotion of both harshness and hidden expectation towards Naoya and the Art Club.
And for Mizuna, her intellect and tenacity are the source of the "quiet" side of Naoya's character. Even though she eventually became Kenichiro's eternal "debt" (life after elopement was always clouded by debt and flight), she never had a single word of complaint. This magnificent value of **"burning out one's whole life for a moment of exquisite beauty"** is precisely the spiritual core passed down through the Kusanagi family.

This chapter also gives the answer to the question in the title:
**If Matter is a shackle so heavy it's suffocating, then Mind is that brilliant cherry blossom that, even if falling into the mud, still dances in the wind.**

## The Happy Prince and Other Tales

The chapter name borrows from Oscar Wilde's poignant fairy tale *The Happy Prince*. In the fairy tale, the prince strips off his gold leaf and gems piece by piece to help the poor, finally becoming dull and ugly and being heartlessly thrown into the furnace.
In this chapter, Naoya is that Happy Prince who has been deprived of everything. To save Rin, save Rina, and save the Natsume girls, he forcibly stripped away his talent, wealth, and future. Naoya stubbornly believed this sacrifice was noble, but reality (represented by Natsume Kei) told him in the cruelest way that this self-moved sacrifice was a desecration of the God of Art.

In that dialogue under the cherry blossom tree between Naoya and Rin, Rin had already keenly sensed that Naoya's "ordinariness" was not a lack of talent, but a free fall he actively chose to catch her. When Rin regained that almost divine talent, she suddenly realized that the "beauty" she carried was built on the sacrifice of squeezing Naoya's marrow and blood. What Naoya conveyed in the conversation was not a shred of a victim's self-pity, but a kind of **"willing self-fading."**
Naoya showed an extreme "Fatherly nature" and "watching." Through that death-match of painting skills in the swimming pool, he proved to the world that even if he became an "ordinary person," he could still touch the edge of art with his broken body. He told Rin that he no longer longed for that altar, and he chose to stay in the mud-filled world. At this time, Rin had already been re-enthroned from the childhood friend "Misaki Rin" to a monster genius with absolute talent. She recognized a cruel fact: true art is destined to be a lonely peak. She won the Moore Prize Platinum Award and became a prophet leading human aesthetics, which also meant she had to cut off the "daily life" full of hearth-fire and bonds where Naoya was.
> "What is called art is that bone-piercing divinity refined after crushing countless ordinary happinesses."

Kei had been painting almost frantically, not for fame and fortune, but to "wait for Naoya to come back." He worshipped Naoya as a god and obsessively believed that only by climbing to a higher height could he force that "god who broke his wings for ordinary people" to descend to the human world again.
In the confrontation with Sui, Naoya realized that even if he lost the "Hand of God" of those years, and even if his right hand could no longer swing freely, he could still complete creation by relying on pointillism, light and shadow, and borrowing others' colors. This determined posture of "comparing a mortal body with a god" is the most unyielding background in the depths of Naoya's soul.
Finally, Naoya picked up the paintbrush again, originally to give Kei a long-overdue explanation, but the joke played by fate was exceptionally cruel—Kei held the trophy as he wished, but died suddenly on the way to the podium.

Upon first reading, one might find Kei's sudden death somewhat absurd, but upon revisiting and reflecting, from a literary structural perspective, it was an inevitable "artistic sacrifice."
The entire meaning of Kei's existence was to "match Naoya." When Naoya picked up the paintbrush again and gave his own answer at the highest level of art, Kei's mission as a "guide" and "fanatic" came to an end. However, art is never warm and tender. Kei's tragic death completely shattered Naoya's naive illusion that "everyone can be happy if they work hard." It coldly told Naoya: **Even if you give everything without reservation, the world may still strip away everything you cherish at the moment you reach the top.** Freeman, the foundation manager, is the embodiment of absolutely objective "aesthetic value." He watched coldly as Kei burned out and also foresaw Rin's spiritual awakening. His existence is like an emotionless referee, watching these geniuses turn to ash on the bloody altar named "art."

In this chapter, Sui's image becomes more three-dimensional than ever before: she is the "miracle" born of Rin's obsession, and the "spirit of art" personally carved by Kenichiro. With Kei's fall and Naoya's setback, Rin's full awakening as the "True God" is unstoppable. Sui disappeared, returning to Rin's body. This means that the simple, fragile childhood friend who needed Naoya's protection completely died, and in her place was born an artist sufficient to rule the world with extreme "beauty" and lead the human soul to dimension-up. Naoya helplessly found that Rin, whom he desperately protected, eventually went to a high dimension that he could not reach even if he spent his whole life. In this chapter, Naoya completed a painful nirvana from a "god" falling to a "human," and then from a "human" sublimating to a "watcher."

Speaking of Ai, at first I merely regarded her as a thin symbol of a "gentle teacher" or "older sister next door," but in this chapter, Ai's role is the toughest hub connecting the past and the future.
Ai was once Kenichiro's student, and she personally witnessed that magnificent yet scarred-all-over life-ending love between Kenichiro and her sister Mizuna. Her meticulous care for Naoya was essentially a silent continuation of Kenichiro and Mizuna's will. When Naoya tried to use "my hand is broken" as a shield to escape from art, Ai was one of the few who could see through the fear in the depths of Naoya's soul at a glance. She didn't press him obsessively like Kei, nor did she worship him blindly like Makoto; she always maintained the inclusive posture of a **"liver,"** quietly waiting for Naoya to complete his psychological reconstruction by his own strength.
Kei's sudden death was undoubtedly a destructive hammer to the Natsume family. as the eldest sister, Ai, in the midst of huge grief, still gritted her teeth to maintain the crumbling operation of the family. She is the embodiment of the words "daily life" in the whole work. When the madness of art tore everything to pieces, it was Ai who firmly grabbed Naoya's clothes and didn't let him slide completely into the abyss of nihility.
Ai's guidance to Naoya in this chapter is the finishing touch. She softly told Naoya not to carry everything alone, which directly prompted Naoya to make up his mind to have that watercolor duel with "Sui." The meaning of her existence is in softly telling Naoya: **Even if you cannot become the "god" who creates miracles, living as a "human" who knows how to perceive beauty is equally full of supreme dignity.**

## A Walk Under the Cherry Blossom Forest

This chapter is by no means an appendage-like epilogue, but the **"homecoming" and "inheritance"** of the entire work at the level of the soul.

In this chapter, Kusanagi Naoya is no longer that genius boy carrying the heavy shadow of his father, with a disabled right hand and even some self-pity. He has become an ordinary art teacher at Yumihari Academy. From a "wanderer" drifting everywhere at first, he has settled into a "watcher" rooted here. Naoya at this time, just like Kenichiro or Natsume Ai back then, transformed into a gentle lighthouse guiding the next generation (Sakurako, Ruriwo, Nene, etc.). What he guards is not only that dilapidated Art Club but also that unyielding artistic soul belonging to Yumihari.

Nagayama Kana represents the **"ordinary person who does her best"** in this chapter. She stubbornly believed that art must be packaged with "commercial value" and "topic hype." Her act of arbitrarily transforming the church murals was essentially modern fast-food art's "plundering" and "overwriting" of traditional deep emotions. Naoya did not pick up the "paintbrush" to have a useless argument with Kana, but cleverly used "design" and "optical principles."
He found the rejected draft manuscripts left by Kenichiro and, combining the extreme analysis of light and shadow, miraculously restored the original grand appearance of the murals at a specific angle with a few simple pieces of cellophane. In this way, Naoya proved to Kana: true art never stops at the smearing of the surface, but is the precise capture of a specific eternal moment across the barrier of time and space.

After the mural storm subsided, Naoya stubbornly attributed all the credit to the deceased Kenichiro. This deeply echoes the most core artistic view of *Sakura no Uta*: "As long as the work conveys the creator's original intention, it has achieved all its value."
Naoya at this time had completely cut off the shackles named "fame." He no longer needed to prove to anyone that he was a genius; he just wanted to quietly guard the traces of existence of those who were vital to him in this world. Rin shone brilliantly in New York and became the darling of the whole world, while Naoya remained unknown in a corner of Yumihari. This extreme disparity in status, on the contrary, sublimated the soul bond of the two to the peak. At the moment she saw the photo, Rin recognized it was Naoya's handiwork at a glance; this resonance in the depths of the soul far exceeds any conspicuous signature in the corner of a painting.

At the end of the story, Naoya and Ai returned to the old courtyard of the Natsume residence. These two are the ones who have been deprived of the most by fate in the whole story: they are not only relatives in name but also the only remaining witnesses of those turbulent years enveloped by madness and miracles. Finally, Naoya found his true "supreme moment"—that was not standing under the spotlight to receive the Moore Prize, but chewing that ordinary yet solid rest of life with the person who understood and accompanied him, year after year in the cherry blossoms and breeze.
Naoya finally lived like that aging "recumbent cherry tree": his roots are deeply buried in the soil named "memory," his trunk is hunched (disability and full of regrets), but in silence, he supports a forest of falling cherry blossoms for the travelers who come after.

## At the End

Looking back after closing the book, *Sakura no Uta* is a grand hymn dedicated to **"people who are destined not to become geniuses, yet still love beauty deeply."** It gently and firmly tells us: although the brilliance of genius is enough to light up the era, those ordinary people who silently grit their teeth and pass on the torch under the strong light (such as Akashi, Makoto, and even Naoya, who finally let go of his obsession) are also using their lives to write the most moving artistic epic.

Just as Naoya confirmed with his life:
**True art is, even in the most barren and desolate land, being able to let that thousand-year-old cherry blossom bloom proudly in the bottom of one's heart with yearning and tenderness.**
